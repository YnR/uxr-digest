## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**[AI in UX: Work Is Faster, But Practitioners Have Mixed Feelings](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-in-ux-survey-26/) — Nielsen Norman Group (Oct 23, 2026)**
Maria Rosala and Rachel Banawa surveyed 521 UX practitioners and found AI access is now near-universal at 99%, while agentic AI use ranges from 85% at startups to 36% at nonprofits. Respondents rated AI's effect on task time at 4.2 out of 5 (where 3 means no change), but feelings were lukewarm overall — output quality (54%) and data privacy and security (50%) were the top-cited barriers. Crucially for researchers, AI use was highest for tasks involving artifacts and data and lowest for tasks involving direct user interaction, suggesting that core research touchpoints — moderation, participant engagement, real-time sense-making — remain the least automated portion of the practice.

**[ResearchOps 2.0: The Future of Research Operations](https://www.userinterviews.com/blog/researchops-and-future-of-research-operations) — User Interviews (Oct 22, 2026)**
User Interviews (now part of Auros, following the UserTesting acquisition) published a retrospective on a five-part audio documentary made with The ChaCha Club and hosted by Kate Towsey. The series traces ResearchOps through three generations — from early operational problem-solvers to practitioners now designing AI-enabled research ecosystems — and surfaces questions that no one has resolved yet: who governs AI-generated research artifacts, and who is accountable when automated workflows shape a product decision. For anyone building or scaling a research operations function, this is a useful orientation on where the field's open problems currently lie.

## Focus Area 3: UX Research Job Market

The NN/g survey above carries a direct job market signal: UX practitioners report speed gains from AI but express mixed feelings, and the tasks farthest from AI adoption are precisely those that define the researcher role — planning and moderating sessions, synthesizing meaning from live interactions, and advising stakeholders on what to do with findings. For practitioners worried about displacement, the data suggests the differentiation strategy is reinforcing depth in those human-contact tasks rather than competing on the speed-and-scale work AI already handles well.
