## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**[Do AI Moderators Uncover as Much as Human Moderators?](https://measuringu.com/do-participants-provide-the-same-depth-of-insight-to-human-and-ai-moderators/) — MeasuringU (Nov 3, 2026)**
A randomized controlled trial with 20 participants found that human moderators surfaced more research themes than AI moderators: humans averaged 6.9 of 8 themes vs. 5.0 for AI, with the gap driven almost entirely by unscripted topics. The most striking miss was a "sleep climate" theme that emerged in six to nine of ten human sessions but zero to one AI sessions — the guide never prompted it, and AI never probed for it. This companion to MeasuringU's earlier post on participant word-count gaps builds a consistent picture: AI moderators hold their own on planned questions but systematically fail to pursue the unexpected tangents where human interviewers generate the highest-value insight. For teams evaluating AI-assisted moderation tools, this study gives the sharpest evidence yet of where the floor is.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**[When A Feature Becomes A Social Rule](https://saeidehbakhshi.substack.com/p/when-a-feature-becomes-a-social-rule) — Research toolbox (Nov 5, 2026)**
Saeideh Bakhshi argues that product features routinely generate social norms their designers never intended: Facebook's relationship-status field turned into a public-commitment ritual, and read receipts created an expectation of immediate response that the feature itself did not demand. Designers control what information a feature makes visible, but have far less control over what people collectively decide that information means. The implications for AI products are direct — when a product surfaces that an AI helped draft a message, or flags AI-generated content, or shows that an agent took an action, users will construct social meanings around those signals that researchers need to track independently of task performance. Standard usability metrics will not catch the normative layer.

**[Berrypicking Still Beats the Single Answer for Search, Even in the World of AI](https://uxdesign.cc/berrypicking-still-beats-the-single-answer-for-search-even-in-the-world-of-ai-6d7174099500) — UX Collective (Nov 5, 2026)**
Applying Marcia Bates's 1989 berrypicking model to conversational AI search, the author argues that a single-answer chat reply conceals exactly the facets, filters, and iterative tactics that help people explore an information space. Twenty-nine of Bates's foraging moves could in principle become UI affordances — but current AI search interfaces hide them behind a prompt box, forcing users to re-derive structure through follow-up questions. For research teams evaluating AI search features, this piece frames a key evaluation gap: measuring whether users find *an* answer misses whether the interface supports the messy, evolving information needs that drive most real-world search.

## Focus Area 3: UX Research Job Market

**[Design by Numbers: Is AI Ruining UX or Just Pouring Gasoline on the Fire?](https://uxdesign.cc/design-by-numbers-is-ai-ruining-ux-or-just-pouring-gasoline-on-the-fire-24164d49e422) — UX Collective (Nov 5, 2026)**
Drawing on Figma's 2026 State of the Designer report — which found 72% of surveyed designers now use generative AI — the piece examines whether AI is degrading design quality, eliminating roles, or simply accelerating existing structural problems. The argument is that AI is compressing the time between ideation and delivery in ways that squeeze out the deliberative space where research findings would normally land. For UX researchers, the signal is not AI adoption itself but the downstream pressure on the discovery and synthesis phases: when design cycles shrink, the work most at risk is the work that cannot happen in parallel with execution.
