## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**AI Can't Replace Real Research in Empathy Mapping** — [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-empathy-mapping/) *(Aug 27–28, 2026)*

When workshop time arrives but user research hasn't been done, teams are increasingly tempted to let AI generate the sticky notes — synthesising "typical" user frustrations from generic training data. NN/g's article makes a direct methodological argument against this: AI can help organise and process research that has already been collected, but it cannot produce evidence about *your* specific users, product failure points, or context. The result, as the piece puts it, is that "plausible isn't the same as real" — AI output describes "a frustrated user of almost any product, which means it describes no one in particular." For UX researchers, this is a useful line to draw in practice: AI as a synthesis aid on collected data is legitimate; AI as a substitute for collecting data is not.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**The Custodial Era of UX: Cleaning Up After AI** — [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-ux-debt/) *(Aug 28, 2026)*

NN/g coins a useful framing for the current moment: teams can now generate content, prototypes, and working features with AI far faster than UX can evaluate them, and the gap accumulates as "UX debt." The work is no longer primarily about designing from a blank page — it is custodial: deciding what the AI produced is worth keeping and what needs to change. NN/g argues UX can respond by building shared judgment criteria across the team, accelerating evaluation processes, and actively guiding AI-generated designs toward quality before they ship. For UX researchers, this reframes the evaluation mandate: rather than evaluating finished designs, researchers increasingly need to be embedded earlier, setting the criteria against which AI output is filtered — essentially writing the brief that the AI is optimising for.
