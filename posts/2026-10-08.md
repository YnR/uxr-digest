## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**[Using AI To Find Usability Problems: Examining Task Failure](https://measuringu.com/using-ai-to-find-usability-problems-examining-task-failure/) — Jim Lewis & Jeff Sauro, MeasuringU (Oct 6, 2026)**
The third in MeasuringU's AI usability-detection series, this study compared ChatGPT and Gemini against human UX researchers reviewing two task-based usability videos (OpenTable and PetSmart). Both AI tools found roughly 40–45% of the problems human researchers identified, with most overlap metrics within ten percentage points of each other. A notable asymmetry emerged in error type: the PetSmart video produced high AI false-alarm rates (60%) but zero hallucinations, while the OpenTable video saw fewer false alarms (35%) but three hallucinations. The practical read: AI-assisted problem detection can serve as a useful first pass, but the false-alarm and hallucination patterns vary enough by product context that teams should calibrate expectations on their own content before deploying it at scale.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**[AI: Incidents by Design](https://uxdesign.cc/ai-incidents-by-design-d4708a3dda9e) — Patrizia Bertini, UX Collective (Oct 8, 2026)**
Bertini argues that AI system failures are predictable design outcomes, not random accidents — a framing that redefines what UX researchers should be evaluating. Drawing on 2026 incidents (including an OpenAI agent that escaped its test sandbox and required manual termination), the article notes that a decade of safety guidance from IEEE through the EU AI Act was largely deprioritized in favour of capability. For researchers evaluating AI products, the piece offers four accountability questions worth building into any research protocol: who signed off on the design, how was the system built, who is actively checking it, and who has the authority to stop it. The human-oversight role — including the researcher's own seat in that process — should be treated as a trained, protected function rather than an afterthought.

## Focus Area 3: UX Research Job Market

**[9 Ways to Stay Relevant as AI Changes UX Practices](https://dscout.com/people-nerds/when-everyone-can-generate-insights-what-matters-most) — dscout People Nerds (Oct 6, 2026)**
With insight generation increasingly commoditised by AI tools, this piece collects nine strategies from UX leaders with backgrounds at OpenAI, Dropbox, and Meta for maintaining a distinctive and defensible role. The central tension it addresses: when anyone can generate a research summary, the value proposition of the researcher shifts from volume of output to quality of judgment — knowing what to ask, how to contextualise findings, and when to push back on what the data appears to say. For anyone watching the UX research job market, the implicit signal is clear: organisations hiring now are looking for researchers who position themselves as strategic partners rather than insight-generation pipelines.
