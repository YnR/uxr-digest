## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**[Can AI Generate Good Designs from Raw User Data?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/raw-data-vs-synthesized-data/) — Nielsen Norman Group (Nov 20, 2026)**
Megan Chan and Rachel Banawa ran a controlled study asking an AI tool to generate interface designs from two inputs: raw user comments versus synthesized findings and recommendations. Designs built from synthesized input satisfied 8.4 percentage points more scorecard criteria on average. The mechanism is straightforward — without synthesis, the AI has to guess what to prioritize, and in the checkout scenario tested, fewer than half of the raw-data designs kept items and totals visible at every step, despite that being one of the most common user complaints. The practical implication for research teams is that synthesis is not just an internal communication step: it is the input that makes AI-generated output usable, which means the analytical judgment work of a skilled researcher is a prerequisite for AI tools to perform well, not something those tools displace.

**[Do AI and Human Moderators Uncover the Same Facts?](https://measuringu.com/do-ai-and-human-moderators-uncover-the-same-facts/) — MeasuringU (Nov 17, 2026)**
The latest entry in MeasuringU's ongoing AI-moderation series compares how much factual screener information AI and human moderators surface during sessions. In a 20-participant randomized study by Jeff Sauro, Lucas Plabst, Eva Sundberg, and Jim Lewis, human moderators uncovered 35% of screener facts versus 21% for AI — primarily because participants volunteered more information unprompted to humans. Word count data from the same team's earlier studies showed participants spoke roughly twice as many words per session to human moderators (2,028 vs. 988), which likely explains much of the gap. This study adds factual retrieval to the previous findings on thematic discovery and verbosity; taken together, the series builds a consistent picture that AI moderation is not yet equivalent to human moderation for depth, while remaining useful for structured or high-volume scenarios where breadth matters more than depth.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**[The Intermediary Comes Back](https://saeidehbakhshi.substack.com/p/the-intermediary-comes-back) — Research toolbox (Nov 20, 2026)**
Saeideh Bakhshi (quantitative UX research lead at OpenAI) argues that software spent decades removing human intermediaries — travel agents, bank tellers, brokers — but that the work those intermediaries did did not disappear; it was transferred to users. AI agents, she argues, could reverse this by acting on a user's behalf rather than on the provider's. The key design research implication is that the classic user-journey assumption — a human user interacting directly with a service interface — may not hold for a growing share of transactions, with the agent navigating the service and the user interacting only with the agent's output. For teams designing and evaluating AI-powered products, this shifts the evaluative question: beyond whether a user can accomplish a task, researchers need to understand whether users can appropriately direct, trust, and correct the agent acting for them.
