## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**One AI Output Is an Example, Not an Evaluation** — [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/eval-ai-output/) *(Aug 14)*

NN/g's new article argues that teams commonly commit a core evaluation error: running an AI system once, inspecting the output, and drawing conclusions about capability. Because AI outputs are probabilistic, a single trial reveals nothing about the system's distribution of behavior — the same prompt can yield an accurate answer, a plausible-but-wrong answer, or a harmful one. The article recommends evaluating with multiple representative inputs, repeated runs, and confidence intervals: the same rigour that research teams expect of user studies should now be applied to AI systems themselves.

**How to Become an AI Designer** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/how-to-become-an-ai-designer-ff8ee8aed205) *(Aug 15)*

Maya Brennan (Lead Designer, Amplitude + Statsig) offers a practitioner's guide to shipping in a world where engineers deploy faster than designers can prototype. The piece is pitched at designers but the structural shift it describes applies equally to researchers: the pace gap between AI-assisted production and traditional hand-crafted work is now the central pressure on workflow design. Researchers considering where to invest in AI tooling will find this a useful signal of where their design-side collaborators are headed.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**Getting Started Is Not Getting It Right** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/getting-started-is-not-getting-it-right-e76270614d14) *(Aug 14)*

Nicole Alexandra Michaelis reports findings from the 2026 Web for All study, which measured AI-generated interfaces against five objectively testable WCAG criteria and found only 29% compliance. More striking: when accessibility requirements were explicitly written into the prompt, compliance got *worse* — suggesting the model was satisficing on stated requirements rather than actually achieving them. For researchers evaluating AI-built products, this is a strong caution against treating prompt-level specification as a proxy for outcome quality; the gap between what an AI system is instructed to do and what it actually delivers needs to be measured empirically.
