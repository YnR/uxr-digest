## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**[A Review of Experiments with Synthetic Users](https://measuringu.com/review-of-experiments-with-synthetic-users/) — MeasuringU (~Sep 24–25, 2026)**
This capstone piece in MeasuringU's synthetic-users series surveys the experimental literature and draws a notably cautious conclusion: synthetic respondents achieve 85–95% distributional similarity to humans on structured tasks (ranking, pricing, sentiment), but consistently show sycophancy bias, majority-opinion convergence, and a near-total inability to produce surprising insights. GPT-4 reproduced the expected factor structure of standardised psychological questionnaires but got the magnitudes wrong; Llama3 failed even the factor structure. The review also highlights a structural limitation: AI participants have no lived biography, which undercuts their usefulness for experiential or longitudinal research. For researchers tempted to use AI surrogates for participant recruitment savings, this piece is the most comprehensive calibration yet of where that strategy holds and where it breaks.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**[When Should You Disclose AI Use? The PACED Framework](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/disclose-ai-paced/) — Nielsen Norman Group (Sep 25, 2026)**
With the EU AI Act's transparency obligations in effect since August 2026, NN/g published a practical decision framework for AI disclosure. PACED stands for Policy, Audience, Context, Expectations, and Degree of AI contribution — five questions that translate the disclosure dilemma into answerable choices for any content type. The article's key empirical grounding is that user reactions to AI disclosure vary significantly by audience and context: the same disclosure that builds trust with one segment damages it with another, so blanket policies are blunt instruments. For researchers designing or evaluating AI-powered products, this framework doubles as a usability heuristic: whether and how a product discloses AI involvement is itself a testable design decision with measurable trust and acceptance consequences.

## Focus Area 3: UX Research Job Market

**[AI in Product Teams: In 2026, the Growing Impact on Collaboration](https://uxdesign.cc/ai-in-product-teams-in-2026-the-growing-impact-on-collaboration-8ea9f6024dca) — Anna Lefour, UX Collective (~Sep 24–25, 2026)**
Lefour's survey data shows a sharp shift from 2025: where last year 77% of designers said AI made them *individually* faster with almost no reported change in cross-functional collaboration, the 2026 picture is different — the impact on how design, engineering, and product work together is now "clearly felt." Designers who lean into AI are 25% more likely to report being happier at work, but the same survey flags that designers record the highest AI-related anxiety and the worst-rated managers of any tech role. The implication for UX researchers watching the job market: AI is beginning to reshape *team structures*, not just individual productivity — which is a richer signal about future role demand than headcount numbers alone.
