## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**How NN/G Uses AI in Its Editorial Process** — [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-editorial-process/) *(Sep 11, 2026)*

NN/g has published a transparent account of how AI is woven into their own content production workflow — one of the more candid institutional disclosures to come from a major UX publisher. Authors may use AI to work through arguments, rephrase dictated thoughts, aid formatting, or serve as a critique partner; the article is explicit that AI may "contribute words and ideas" to published pieces. The firm draws a firm line at editorial responsibility: human experts with relevant expertise decide whether an argument is sound and ultimately stand behind every published claim. For UX research teams formalising their own AI-assisted workflows, this piece is useful less as a template and more as a benchmark — it articulates a principled, documented position on AI participation that research organisations can adapt when defining their own standards for AI-assisted synthesis, report writing, or insight communication.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**Persistent lessons in human-centered automation** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/persistent-lessons-in-human-centered-automation-45b53412e6a5) *(Sep 2026)*

Meg Kurdziolek draws a direct line from Lucy Suchman's landmark 1983 photocopier study — which showed that real user behaviour systematically diverged from the step-by-step plans assumed by the machine — to the design and evaluation challenges posed by today's agentic AI systems. The core argument is that probabilistic, context-sensitive AI reasoning inherits and amplifies the same breakdown points that tripped up 1980s automation: systems that model an idealised user action sequence rather than the messy, repair-heavy reality of how people actually complete goals. For researchers conducting studies on agentic AI products, the piece offers a concrete analytical frame: looking for the moments where the system's assumed plan and the user's actual repair work diverge is a generalisable method for surfacing failure modes that standard task-completion metrics will miss.
