## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**Artificial Intelligence Glossary** — [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/artificial-intelligence-glossary/) *(Aug 21)*

NN/g published a plain-language glossary of AI terms — tokens, context windows, agents, evals, prompt injection, and more — written specifically for people doing product and design work rather than for ML engineers. The definitions follow how terms are actually used in AI products and UX practice, which often diverges from how vendors and researchers use the same words. For UX researchers, this is a directly practical resource: as AI features become standard on research roadmaps, shared vocabulary with engineers and PMs reduces the friction of translating between disciplines, and having working definitions for concepts like "evals" helps researchers argue for proper AI output evaluation rather than one-off demos.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**IA for AI, The UX of Contrast, Battling AI Fatigue** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/ia-for-ai-the-ux-of-contrast-battling-ai-fatigue-4f623495bed8) *(Aug 2026)*

Fabricio Teixeira's weekly curated roundup centers on the argument that information architecture is the discipline AI products are currently starving for — AI systems that lack clear content hierarchy and semantic structure produce unpredictable, unreliable outputs because the underlying information is poorly organized, not because the model is weak. The issue also covers AI fatigue: 2026 is increasingly described as the year users are developing blanket skepticism toward AI features, posing a new research challenge — distinguishing fatigue from legitimate usability failure in studies of AI products. Researchers evaluating AI features should now routinely probe whether negative reactions are driven by the feature itself, its design, or broader accumulation of AI disappointments.
