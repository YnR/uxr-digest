## 1. AI in the Research Practice

**[How to Decide When an AI Tool Is Worth Keeping — NN/G (Aug 7)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/prove-framework/)**
NN/G published a practical evaluation framework called PROVE (Problem Alignment, Risk, Output Quality, Velocity, Experience) for deciding whether an AI tool deserves a permanent place in your workflow. The method is deliberately scoped: one tool, one task, one provisional decision — not an org-wide rollout recommendation. The article walks through a live example using Google's Gemini Notebooks and ends with the advice to treat the outcome as revisable, since both tools and tasks evolve. Useful scaffolding for researchers under pressure to rapidly adopt AI tooling.

**[Can We Trust AI to Moderate UX Interviews? — MeasuringU (Aug 4)](https://measuringu.com/can-we-trust-ai-to-moderate-ux-interviews/)**
Jeff Sauro, Lucas Plabst, and Jim Lewis examine the specific case of AI-moderated user interviews — a capability that has advanced enough to attract professional concern (419 researchers have signed open letters opposing it). The piece is measured rather than alarmist: it asks where AI moderation adds value (structured, repeatable tasks like job interview simulations) versus where the loss of human judgment matters most (unstructured exploratory research). The framing is a useful forcing function for teams deciding where human moderators are irreplaceable.

## 2. Research for AI-Powered Products

**[IA for AI, The UX of Contrast, Battling AI Fatigue — UX Collective / Fabricio Teixeira (August 2026)](https://uxdesign.cc/ia-for-ai-the-ux-of-contrast-battling-ai-fatigue-4f623495bed8)**
This week's UX Collective newsletter curates articles on information architecture for AI systems, the emerging design challenge of AI fatigue among users, and how to structure AI-product experiences that sustain engagement rather than exhaust it. AI fatigue is becoming a design research problem in its own right: as AI features proliferate, how users experience the cumulative weight of AI interactions — and when they disengage — needs dedicated study. Research teams working on AI products should consider adding fatigue and trust calibration to their standard measurement set.

**[Evaluating AI-Generated Research Output — dscout People Nerds (late July)](https://dscout.com/people-nerds/the-new-ux-tech-stack)**
A dscout webinar featuring research leaders from The Cigna Group, Datadog, and TD Bank Group explored how enterprise teams govern trust in AI-assisted workflows. The recurring theme: organizations are converging on a specialist model — dedicated researchers who own AI output quality control — rather than a model where everyone dabbles. For teams standing up AI research workflows, this is a practical signal about how mature organizations are structuring oversight.

## 3. Job Market for UX Researchers

**[The Ultimate UX Research Job Board — Updated August 2026 (User Interviews)](https://www.userinterviews.com/blog/the-ultimate-remote-ux-research-job-board)**
The latest job board update from User Interviews reflects a continued bifurcation: entry-level opportunities have contracted while senior and strategic roles hold steady or grow. Hiring signals in 2026 cluster around three profiles: researchers with strong AI tool fluency, those who can directly translate research into business decisions, and those who can lead or oversee AI-assisted research operations. The broader market context (from multiple industry sources) is sobering — 35% of organizations reported reducing UX staff this year, the highest rate on record per UXPA — but the demand for researchers who can demonstrate value above what AI can automate is stable.
