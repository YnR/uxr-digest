## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**AI Has a Hospitality Problem Money Can't Fix** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/ai-has-a-hospitality-problem-money-cant-fix-e8fa34e91d62) *(Aug 2026)*

Dan Maccarone argues that as raw AI intelligence becomes a commodity — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini converging on "good enough" — the only remaining differentiator is the quality of the relationship the product builds with the user. Drawing on hospitality expert Danny Meyer's definition of service ("a monologue") versus hospitality ("a dialogue"), Maccarone makes the case that what nobody has built yet is "the AI you'd actually come back to because the last time you used it, it felt like something was finally on your side." For UX researchers, this reframes a key evaluation question: usability and task-completion metrics capture whether an AI product works, but not whether it makes users feel understood — a dimension that may increasingly determine retention and trust. Research designs for AI products likely need a new layer of relational quality assessment beyond standard task success rates.

**We're Gorging on Borrowed Trust and It's Going to Cost Us** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/were-gorging-on-borrowed-trust-and-it-s-going-to-cost-us-2bc3f918caf5) *(Aug 2026)*

Also from Maccarone: AI products are currently capitalising on the accumulated reputational trust of the brands hosting them — users extend to "Google AI" or "Microsoft Copilot" the trust they built over decades with those names. The argument is that this is borrowed capital, not earned, and that the more users trust the name, the less they interrogate the machine wearing it — creating the conditions for a significant trust collapse when AI-powered decisions go wrong. For UX researchers studying AI-powered products, this framing sharpens a specific research risk: survey and attitudinal data about AI trust may be capturing brand halo rather than genuine trust in the AI's outputs. Separating those signals in research design — for example by using unfamiliar or fictional AI brands in controlled studies — becomes methodologically important.
