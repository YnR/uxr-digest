## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**[Should You Actually Add AI To That? A Four-Question Audit For Product Teams](https://smashingmagazine.com/2026/11/should-you-add-ai-audit-product-teams/) — Smashing Magazine (via UX Design Weekly Issue 615, Nov 23, 2026)**
Victor Yocco, PhD (author of *Designing Agentic AI Experiences*, CRC Press 2026) offers a decision framework for product teams deciding whether and where to add AI to their products. The first audit question — does the feature actually reduce total effort for the user? — is illustrated with a scenario of a health insurer whose AI writes full benefits-appeal determinations: the technology works, but the user burden question is left unanswered. The framework is explicitly oriented around user experience of effort rather than technical capability, which makes it useful as a research evaluation lens independent of the product role the reader holds. For UX researchers, the article operationalises a question that often gets skipped in discovery: before testing how well an AI feature works, teams should be able to demonstrate they have answered why it was added in the first place and whose effort it was meant to reduce.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**[Shopping in the AI Era: How the Holiday Hunt Has Changed](https://dscout.com/people-nerds/shopping-in-the-ai-era-how-the-holiday-hunt-has-changed) — People Nerds (dscout) (Nov 23, 2026)**
Dscout surveyed 100 shoppers on how AI fits into their 2026 holiday shopping, and the headline finding marks a clear delegation boundary: shoppers want AI to handle discovery and comparison work but still want to retain control of the final purchase — "handle the legwork, keep the checkout button." The finding has a direct implication for research on agentic commerce: willingness to delegate an action to AI is not uniform along the task sequence, so measuring overall comfort with AI shopping assistance will produce a misleading signal. For teams designing or evaluating AI-assisted shopping flows, the distinction between assisted browsing and delegated purchasing is a testable threshold, not a binary preference, and study designs should probe where along the task chain user control becomes non-negotiable.
