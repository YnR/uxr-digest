## Focus Area 1: AI in the Research Practice

**NNG's PROVE Framework for AI Tool Evaluation** — [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/prove-framework/) *(Aug 7)*

NN/g published a lightweight framework called PROVE for deciding whether an AI tool is worth keeping in a workflow: Problem alignment, Risk, Output quality, Velocity, and Experience. It tests one tool against one specific task and produces a defensible, repeatable evaluation. For UX researchers under pressure to adopt AI tools quickly, this gives a structured method to assess tools on evidence rather than hype — and the same logic applies to evaluating AI-powered research platforms.

**Designers Are Becoming Toolmakers** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/designs-dreaded-phrase-is-coming-back-this-time-we-re-in-control-ab0c6996dc1e) *(Aug 12)*

Drawing on the 2026 AI in Design report, Kai Wong argues that practitioners are shifting from tool users to toolmakers — building bespoke tools and encoding their judgment, taste, and process into custom AI-augmented workflows. This mirrors an emerging arc in research operations: the next competitive advantage is not which off-the-shelf AI tool a team uses, but whether they've embedded their methodological standards into the infrastructure itself.

## Focus Area 2: Research for AI-Powered Products

**Trust Transfer and the Risk of Borrowed Credibility** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/were-gorging-on-borrowed-trust-and-it-s-going-to-cost-us-2bc3f918caf5) *(Aug 13)*

Dan Maccarone writes about how users apply brand trust to AI systems without scrutinizing the machine itself — the more trusted the name, the less they question the output. The piece has direct implications for evaluating AI features: standard satisfaction metrics will not surface the gap between felt trust and warranted trust. Researchers studying AI products need methods that specifically probe trust calibration, not just task success or satisfaction scores.

**A Droid Model for AI Product Research** — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/what-star-wars-got-right-about-ai-6404ae7c2e62) *(Aug 11)*

Patrick Neeman argues that the Star Wars droid model — cheap, specialized, everywhere, slightly annoying — maps more accurately to today's AI than the "general assistant" framing. For researchers, this is a useful corrective when designing studies: rather than evaluating a single AI as if it operates alone, the question becomes how users navigate fleets of narrow, task-specific AI agents with overlapping functions and inconsistent personalities.
